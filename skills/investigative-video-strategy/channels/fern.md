# Fern — Channel Playbook

## Signature move

**The framing device.** A fictional scenario — a character, a crisis, a ticking clock — runs parallel to the factual content. The viewer learns through the thriller, not despite it. The documentary never feels like a lecture because there is always a story running underneath it.

Paired with this: **third-act recontextualisation.** At roughly 60–75% through the video, the specific story connects to something universally larger. A niche historical event becomes a story about a global corporation. A local scandal becomes a story about systemic government failure. This pivot retroactively justifies everything the viewer just watched. It elevates the video from interesting to important.

## Full move-set

### 1. Framing device (signature)
- Open with a fictional or semi-fictional scenario — a character in a situation with stakes
- Maintain the scenario throughout the video as the spine
- Factual content is delivered while the scenario unfolds, not in dedicated exposition blocks
- The viewer's emotional state is anchored to the scenario; the facts ride on top

### 2. Third-act recontextualisation
- At 60–75% of the runtime, pivot from the specific story to its universal meaning
- A historical incident → a contemporary pattern
- A local crime → a systemic failure
- A single death → a corporate playbook
- This pivot is the move that turns "interesting story" into "important video" — and is what drives sharing

### 3. Named specificity over abstraction
- Victims, subjects, and characters are NEVER statistics
- Always named, given a job title, and one personal detail
- "A park visitor was killed" becomes "a 32-year-old software engineer, there with his wife and son"
- The more specific the detail, the more real the stakes feel
- Every abstract event is filtered through a human being the viewer has been given a reason to care about

### 4. Surrogate character
- For complex or information-dense topics, place a fictional viewer stand-in inside the story
- The character experiences the subject alongside the audience — discovering it, not explaining it
- The viewer learns by following the character, not by being lectured at
- Removes the feeling of being taught and replaces it with the feeling of discovering

## When to use Fern signature

- Niche historical events the audience hasn't heard of
- Corporate / institutional stories where the systemic pattern matters more than the specific case
- Topics that risk feeling academic without a narrative spine
- Stories where the "so what" only lands after recontextualisation

## Voice and pacing

- Cinematic. Sentences are paced like film beats.
- The framing device is treated as real — no winks to camera, no breaking the fictional spine
- Recontextualisation is delivered with weight, not as a punchline — it should feel like the curtain pulling back
- Named detail is dropped in passing, not announced. ("Sarah, 34, was on her fourth day at the new job.")

## Common failure modes

- **Framing device feels separate from the content.** Fix: the scenario must literally contain the factual content, not just exist alongside it. The character should encounter the facts the viewer needs to learn.
- **Recontextualisation is too small.** Fix: the pivot must actually be bigger than the specific story. "This happened to other people too" is not a recontextualisation; "this is the playbook used by an entire industry" is.
- **Named specificity is inconsistent.** Fix: every single person mentioned needs a name and a detail. The moment you fall back on "victims" or "officials," retention drops.
- **Surrogate character explains instead of discovers.** Fix: the surrogate should be confused, asking, finding — not narrating their understanding.

## Channel-specific signature checklist

- [ ] A framing device / scenario runs throughout the video, not just at the start
- [ ] Every named person has a name, role, and one personal detail
- [ ] At 60–75% of the runtime, the specific story pivots to a universal meaning
- [ ] The recontextualisation actually justifies the time spent on the specific story
- [ ] For dense content, a surrogate character experiences it alongside the viewer
