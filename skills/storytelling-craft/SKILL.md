# Storytelling Craft

A skill for engineering attention. Built from one principle: **a story is the intentional space between what the viewer knows and what they want to know.** Everything below is the machinery for building, holding, and closing that space.

## When to use this skill

Use it for: video scripts (long-form, Shorts, Reels, TikTok), voiceovers, narrative-driven posts, explainer content, "how does X work" pieces, retention rewrites, hook audits, and post-mortems on content that underperformed.

Do NOT use it for: pure reference documentation, code comments, transactional copy (receipts, confirmations), or anything where the reader is already committed and doesn't need to be pulled forward.

**Edge case — narrative writing that isn't video** (eulogies, essays, toasts, story-driven blog posts): the *principles* of this skill still apply — curiosity gap, friction reduction, visceral words, withheld payoff — but skip the [VISUAL]/VO column format, which only makes sense when there's an actual visual track. Treat the visual cues as paragraph-level pacing markers instead, or drop them entirely.

## The Prime Directive: The Curiosity Gap

A story is not a list of facts in a pleasing order. A story is a controlled tension between two points:

```
[ What the viewer ALREADY knows ]  ───►  [ What they want to know next ]
                              ▲
                      THE CURIOSITY GAP
                  (this is what you are building)
```

If there is no gap, there is no story — there is just information, and information loses to anything more interesting on the next swipe. Everything in this skill is in service of opening that gap, keeping it open, and closing it with a payoff that feels earned.

A useful test: if you can tell the viewer the punchline in the first 5 seconds and they still want to watch, you have not built a gap — you have built a summary.

## The three moves of any good story

Every retention-driven piece runs this loop. Memorize it.

### 1. The Hook (roughly the first 10–20 seconds)

The hook is not "the first 3 seconds." It is the entire stretch of time during which the viewer is deciding whether to stay. That can be 15–20 seconds for a long-form video. Treat the whole opening as the hook.

The hook does three jobs at once:

- **Names the question.** Phrase it as something the viewer didn't realize they wanted to know. Not "today we'll talk about pens" — "this pen sits in a museum, and I want to know why."
- **Builds authority.** Tell the viewer why your answer will be worth waiting for. "I drove to a cell tower." "I read every patent filed in 1953." "I held the original in my hand." This is the receipt that justifies the runtime. Without it, the viewer assumes you're guessing and leaves.
- **Promises a payoff, then withholds it.** Frame the answer as something specific and concrete that's coming — don't deliver any part of it yet.

If your hook gives away the answer, you have written a headline, not a hook.

**Hook-craft moves that work** (use one or stack them):

- **Specificity over abstraction.** "A pen" is dead. "This pen, which costs 87 cents and sits behind glass at MoMA" is alive. Numbers, brand names, specific objects, named places — these all signal "this is a real thing, not a vibe."
- **Juxtaposition.** Pair two things that don't belong together. Cheapest pen / museum. Nuclear submarine / groceries. Tiny mechanism / 20-minute morning ritual it replaced. The friction between the two halves is the gap.
- **Violated expectation.** State something the viewer assumes is true, then signal that it isn't. "You think airplane windows are round for the view. They're round because square ones killed 56 people."
- **Visceral verb choice.** Words have texture. "I touched the original" hits harder than "I saw the original." "Shouting at the tower" hits harder than "transmitting to the tower." When you have a choice between a clinical word and a body-word, pick the body-word. The viewer feels it.

### 2. The Context (the body)

This is where most scripts die. The context section is not the boring middle to get through — it IS the story. The payoff at the end is only as satisfying as the context that earned it.

Rules for context that holds:

- **Use 5th-grade words.** If a viewer has to stop and parse a word, they're gone. Replace every technical term with the simplest accurate equivalent. "Frequency modulation interference" becomes "the phone keeps shouting at the tower." "Capillary action" becomes "the ink climbs up like a paper towel soaking water." Jargon is friction, and friction kills retention.
- **Build in chronological/causal order.** A → B → C → so therefore D. Viewers can follow a chain. They cannot follow a web.
- **Every beat must name a visual.** Don't write "I explain how phones connect." Write "[holding paper airplane, camera low angle] phones connect by shouting at towers." If you can't picture what's on screen, neither can the viewer.
- **Withhold the answer until the context peaks.** Giving the answer in the middle is the most common script-killing mistake. The body's job is to make the question feel bigger, not to resolve it.

### 3. The Punchline (the close)

Land the answer **after** the context has peaked, not before. The reveal should feel inevitable in hindsight — like the viewer almost figured it out themselves. That feeling is the payoff.

A clean punchline:

- Resolves the exact question posed in the hook (not a different one).
- Uses one concrete image or sentence the viewer will remember.
- Stops. Don't add a recap. Don't summarize what you just said. The viewer just lived it.

## Pacing by runtime

The three-move loop scales, but the beat allocation shifts dramatically by format. Use this as a starting point, not gospel:

| Format | Total | Hook | Context | Punchline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TikTok / Reel / Short | 15–60s | 0–3s | 3s → end-2s | last 2s |
| YouTube Short | 60s | 0–10s | 10–55s | 55–60s |
| Mid-form YouTube | 3–8 min | 0–20s | 20s → ~85% | last 10–20s |
| Long-form YouTube | 10–20 min | 0–45s | 45s → ~90% | last 30–60s |

Two things to notice. First, the hook eats a larger *percentage* of the runtime for short-form — on a TikTok, the first 3 seconds is the whole game, and your authority move has to happen in the same breath as your question ("This pen sits in a museum — here's why"). Second, on long-form, the context section is enormous, which is why long videos die without sub-gaps along the way. Open a small loop every 60–90 seconds inside the big one to keep tension alive.

## Anti-patterns: the named script-killers

Most flat content fails the same five ways. Name them so you can spot them in your own drafts.

- **The Topic Trap.** Opening with "Today we'll talk about X." This is a syllabus, not a story. There's no question, no gap, nothing pulling the viewer forward. **Fix:** Replace with a specific question the topic answers.
- **The Premature Reveal.** Stating the punchline in the hook because you're worried the viewer will leave if you don't promise enough. This kills the gap and leaves you with 90 seconds of redundant context. **Fix:** Trust the question. The gap is the promise.
- **The Wikipedia Voice.** Stringing together facts in chronological order without a unifying question. The viewer can tell they're being read at, not told a story. **Fix:** Pick one question. Every fact must move you closer to its answer.
- **The Floating Voiceover.** Narration over generic stock b-roll. The visuals are doing zero work. The viewer's eye glazes because nothing on screen rewards looking. **Fix:** Specify a visual for every beat, ideally physical and in-frame.
- **The Recap Outro.** Ending with "so today we learned X, Y, and Z." The viewer just lived it — recapping is an admission that you don't trust them to have absorbed it. **Fix:** Land the punchline. Stop.

## Friction reduction: the invisible craft

Most "boring" videos aren't boring because the topic is boring. They're boring because the viewer is doing math in their head trying to follow along. Your job is to do that math for them.

Apply this checklist to every paragraph you write:

- **Jargon swap.** Any word a 10-year-old wouldn't know — swap or define inline with a metaphor.
- **No nested clauses.** Short sentences. One idea per sentence.
- **Visual anchor every 5–8 seconds.** If the viewer's eye has nothing new to land on, their thumb gets bored.
- **Read it aloud.** If you trip over a sentence, the viewer will too. Rewrite until it flows in one breath.

## Coupling words to visuals (the scripting format)

Write scripts in two columns — what is said, and what is shown. The transcript this skill is built from is explicit about this: every line of voiceover should name its accompanying visual. Even if you output prose, mark the visual cues inline.

**Use this format:**

```
[VISUAL: high angle, holding a paper airplane against a white wall]
VO: Your phone is basically shouting. And the tower is the only thing
    in the sky that can shout back.

[VISUAL: close-up of phone, signal bars flickering]
VO: But put 200 phones on one plane, all shouting at once...

[VISUAL: cut to cell tower, drone shot]
VO: ...and the tower can't hear any of them.
```

The visual is not decoration. It is half the story. When the words and the visual reinforce each other, retention compounds. When they fight (generic stock b-roll over specific narration), retention collapses.

A useful trick from the source material: **custom physical props beat stock b-roll**. A homemade paper airplane in your hand is more arresting than a clip of a 737 you pulled from Pexels, because it keeps the viewer locked in *your* world rather than reminding them of the wider internet.

## Diagnosing a story that isn't working

When the user hands you a script or video that flopped, run this diagnostic in order. Stop at the first failure — that's almost always the root cause.

1. **Is there a curiosity gap at all?** Read the first 20 seconds. Can you say what *specific question* the video is going to answer? Not "the topic is coffee" — the topic is not a question. The question is "why did one drink, out of every plant humans ever chewed on, become the thing the entire working world now runs on?" If you can only name a topic, the video has no gap and no story. This is the most common failure by a wide margin — about 80% of flopped explainer content dies here. **Fix:** Throw out the opening. Rewrite it as a single specific question the viewer didn't know they wanted answered.
2. **Does the hook give away the answer?** If the first 20 seconds already contain the punchline, the rest of the video is competing with itself. Move the answer to the end.
3. **Is there authority in the hook?** Find the sentence that tells the viewer "trust me, the answer is worth waiting for." If it doesn't exist, retention collapses around the 30-second mark.
4. **Does the context use jargon?** Scan for any word a 10-year-old wouldn't know. Each one is a retention cliff.
5. **Do the visuals match the words?** If the script could be played over any generic b-roll, the visuals are doing zero work. Specify them.
6. **Does the punchline land where the context peaks?** Or does the energy drop before the answer arrives? If it drops, cut whatever is between the peak and the reveal.

## Metrics that actually mean something

When the user asks "is this good?" — direct their attention away from likes. The transcript this skill is built from is explicit: comments are signal (people felt enough to type), shares are the ultimate compliment (they put their reputation on the line by passing it on). Likes are the cheapest currency. Optimize the script for sharability and discussion, not approval.

## Worked example: the Bic pen video

This is the pattern the skill is encoding. Study it.

**Hook (authority + gap):** "This pen sits in a museum. Not because it's expensive — it costs less than a dollar. So why is this specific, cheapest possible pen, the one humans decided was worth preserving forever?"

**Context (chronological, jargon-free, visually anchored):**
- Bamboo reeds dipped in ink — messy.
- Bird feathers carved into quills — broke constantly.
- 1800s fountain pens — leaked in every pocket.
- (Each beat named on screen with the actual object held in hand or a tight visual.)

**Punchline:** "The Bic was the first pen that did the one thing pens are supposed to do — work — without asking anything of you. That's why it's in a museum. Not because it's special. Because it was the first one that wasn't."

Note: the answer ("it just works reliably") would be a boring sentence on its own. The 90 seconds of failed pens before it is what makes that sentence land.

## Worked example 2: the airplane mode video (a "why" question, not a "history" question)

The Bic example was historical. This one shows the same pattern applied to a present-tense technical question — proving the structure is format-agnostic.

**Hook (authority + violated expectation):** "I went to a cell tower to figure this out. You've been told airplane mode is about safety. It isn't. It's about a problem that has nothing to do with the plane."

Notice: the authority move ("I went to a cell tower") and the violated expectation ("it isn't about safety") are stacked in three sentences. On short-form, you stack; on long-form, you stretch.

**Context (jargon-free, chronological, visually anchored):**
- [Holding paper airplane, low angle] Your phone is constantly shouting at the nearest tower.
- [Cell tower drone shot] The tower decides which phone to listen to based on which is closest.
- [Cut back to paper airplane, raised higher] Now put your phone 30,000 feet up. Suddenly it can see hundreds of towers at once. And so can the 200 other phones on the plane.
- [Towers blinking on a map] The towers get overwhelmed trying to figure out which phone belongs where. The whole local network jams.

**Punchline:** "Airplane mode isn't protecting the plane. It's protecting the people on the ground who can't make a call because you're up there shouting at every tower in three states at once."

What to learn from the contrast: the Bic video used *time* as its context engine (history of pens). The airplane-mode video uses *scale* (one phone vs. 200 phones at altitude). Both work because they keep adding pressure to the question until the answer feels like a release.

## The self-check loop (run this before delivering)

Before you hand any script or rewrite back to the user, run it through these checks. If any check fails, revise before delivering.

- [ ] Can I state the curiosity gap in one sentence? (What does the viewer know vs. want to know?)
- [ ] Does the hook build authority — is there a specific reason to trust the answer?
- [ ] Is the answer withheld until the context peaks?
- [ ] Are all words at a 5th-grade reading level, or substituted with a metaphor?
- [ ] Does every beat name a specific visual?
- [ ] If this is a script, did I actually use the [VISUAL] / VO format? (It is easy to forget under deadline pressure and revert to prose.)
- [ ] Would I keep watching past the 20-second mark? Past the 60-second mark?
- [ ] Does the punchline resolve the exact question posed in the hook?

If you can't answer yes to all of these, the script isn't done — keep tuning.

## Output formats

**Before writing: clarify only when you must.** If the user gave you a topic and a length, just write. Don't ping-pong them with five clarifying questions — that's friction in your own deliverable. Ask only when the request is genuinely ambiguous in a way that would change the structure: unclear platform (a 15-second TikTok and a 12-minute YouTube are different animals), unknown audience expertise level (matters for jargon calibration), or a missing payoff (if the user says "make a video about pens" but doesn't know the angle, *that* is worth surfacing — you can't build a gap without one). Otherwise, ship a draft and let them react to something concrete.

**For a script request:** Output in the two-column [VISUAL]/VO format above. Include timestamps if the user gave a target length.

**For a hook rewrite:** Give 2–3 versions, each labeled with the gap it builds and the authority move it makes. Let the user pick.

**For a diagnostic:** Walk the 6-step diagnostic above. State which step the script fails at, then propose the specific fix.

**For an explainer/teaching response:** Don't just tell the user the principle — show it. If you're explaining curiosity gaps, write a tiny example that demonstrates one. The skill is teaching by example as much as by rule.
